1.1Constructing Noisy Training Inputs

I center-crop the sunset photograph in assets/iso.jpeg to 512 × 512, normalize RGB to $[-1,1]$, and reuse one noise tensor with seed 180. Interpolation attenuates the photograph while adding noise:

\[x_t=(1-t)\epsilon+t x_1,\qquad \epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I).\]
def interpolate(image, noise, t):
    return (1 - t) * noise + t * image

At $t=0.25$, the scene is heavily obscured. Increasing $t$ reveals the sunset, swing chains, horizon, and silhouette. Keeping the noise fixed makes the later denoising comparisons use exactly the same inputs. As $t$ increases toward 1, $x_t$ approaches the ground-truth image $g=x_1$: the noise coefficient $1-t$ decreases to zero, while the image coefficient $t$ approaches one.

1.2Classical Denoising

Before applying a Gaussian filter, I divide by $t$ to restore the image amplitude. I try $\sigma\in\{1,2,4,8,16\}$ and select the lowest reconstruction MSE for each input. This selection uses the known clean image.

\[\hat x_1^{\mathrm{blur}}=G_\sigma(x_t/t).\]
from torchvision.transforms.functional import gaussian_blur

def gaussian_denoise(x, t, sigma):
    kernel_size = int(6 * sigma + 1)
    return gaussian_blur(
        x / t,
        [kernel_size, kernel_size],
        [sigma, sigma],
    )

Blur suppresses the random color variation, but also softens the swing chains and the boundary of the silhouette. At low $t$, restoring the image amplitude also amplifies the noise, making the tradeoff between smoothing and detail especially visible.

I choose $\sigma$ independently for each noisy input by comparing the pixelwise MSE of all five filtered candidates against the clean image. The selected values are $\sigma=16$ at $t=0.25$, $\sigma=4$ at $t=0.50$, and $\sigma=2$ at $t=0.75$. After dividing by $t$, the remaining noise has scale $(1-t)/t$, so lower $t$ favors stronger smoothing, while higher $t$ allows a smaller blur width to preserve detail.

1.3One-Step Denoising

With the prompt a high quality photo, one velocity prediction extrapolates from the current time to the endpoint:

\[\hat x_1=x_t+(1-t)v_\theta(x_t,t,c).\]

The learned model restores coherent color regions and scene boundaries more effectively than a local blur. A single prediction still loses fine structure: at $t=0.50$, the water and the small swing-chain details are smoother than in the original.

1.4Visualizing Velocity

The ground-truth path velocity is $v_{\mathrm{gt}}=x_1-\epsilon$. I compare the predicted endpoint update with the exact update, then compute the RGB RMS error at each pixel before any clipping or display scaling.

\[\Delta_{\mathrm{pred}}=(1-t)v_\theta,\quad \Delta_{\mathrm{gt}}=x_1-x_t,\quad E_t=\sqrt{\operatorname{mean}_{RGB}[(\Delta_{\mathrm{pred}}-\Delta_{\mathrm{gt}})^2]}.\]

All six RGB updates share one symmetric signed scale, with zero represented by middle gray. The error heatmaps share the linear interval $[0,0.7]$; values above the upper bound saturate. Differences show where the learned update departs from the exact reconstruction of this particular photograph.

The differences between the predicted and ground-truth updates are concentrated along the boundary between the person and the background, around the small openings in the swing's metal chain links, and in the fine ripples on the sea. These sharp boundaries and fine textures contain high-frequency information that is obscured by the added noise. This makes the original details difficult to distinguish from random fluctuations, so the model predicts the updates less accurately in these regions.

1.5Iterative Denoising with Euler Sampling

Using one fixed direction assumes that a local velocity prediction remains valid along the entire path. However, the model learns a marginal velocity field: at each noisy state $x_t$, time $t$, and prompt $c$, it predicts a posterior-weighted average of the sample-specific conditional velocities compatible with that state. Although each training pair follows a straight path with constant velocity $x_1-\epsilon$, this average need not equal the conditional velocity for the particular source image. As the state and time change, the weights also change, so the learned trajectory can curve. I therefore integrate in small time steps and recompute the velocity after each update. The last step is shortened when necessary to end at $t=1$.

\[x_{t+h}=x_t+h\,v_\theta(x_t,t,c),\qquad h=\min(0.02,1-t).\]
while t < 1:
    h = min(step_size, 1 - t)
    x = x + h * velocity(x, t)
    t += h
    trajectory.append((t, x))

Starting from the saved $t=0.50$ input takes 25 updates. Below are the initial image and every fifth update, including the endpoint.

Reconstruction error and perceived detail describe different properties: a plausible texture need not reproduce the exact pixels of the original photograph. The constant-velocity checks also pass for 50 full steps, 25 half-interval steps, and a shortened final step.

2.1Classifier-Free Guidance

We use classifier-free guidance (CFG) to guide generation with a text condition. At the same image $x_t$ and time $t$, the model predicts a conditional velocity $v_c$ and an unconditional velocity $v_u$. Their difference captures how the text condition changes the predicted direction; adding a scaled version of this difference to $v_c$ strengthens the influence of the prompt without requiring a separate classifier. We compare ordinary conditional sampling at $w=0$ with guided sampling at $w=7$. Here, the conditional prompt is a high quality photo, and the unconditional prompt is the empty string "".

\[v_{\mathrm{CFG}}=v_c+w(v_c-v_u),\qquad w=7.\]

Part A · Creative Applications

Editing Images, Combining Interpretations

3.1Image-to-Image Editing

Image-to-image editing uses an existing image as the starting point for generating a new version. By partially corrupting the input with noise and then running the learned flow toward a clean image, we allow the model to reinterpret its content under the prompt a high quality photo. We expect the output to preserve recognizable aspects of the original composition while adding plausible photographic details. The starting time controls how much the image can change: smaller $t_{\mathrm{start}}$ permits stronger transformations, while larger values preserve more of the original structure.

\[x_{t_{\mathrm{start}}}=(1-t_{\mathrm{start}})\epsilon+t_{\mathrm{start}}x_1.\]

Very small starting times allow large changes because little source information survives. The Campanile becomes other vertical structures, the sunset scene changes subjects, and the pixel-art dog initially becomes unrelated photographic content. At larger starting times, the tower, swing scene, and pixel-art face are increasingly preserved.

Editing Three Web Images

I repeat image-to-image editing on three web images: a rooftop illustration, a stone building at night, and an illustrated forest campfire. Each input is center-cropped and resized to 512 × 512. The panels show the original followed by edits at $t_{\mathrm{start}}=0.02,0.04,0.06,0.10,0.14,0.25,0.50$, in reading order. All three sweeps use seed 180, the prompt a high quality photo, CFG weight $w=7$, and Euler step size $h=0.02$.

Small starting times produce large semantic changes, including unrelated portraits and objects. At $t=0.25$ and $0.50$, the rooftop, nighttime building, and forest campfire become recognizable again. Higher starting times preserve more of the original layouts and color palettes; the illustrated inputs also keep much of their drawn style despite the photographic prompt.

class Conv(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, in_channels: int, out_channels: int):
        super().__init__()
        self.conv = nn.Conv2d(in_channels, out_channels, 3, 1, 1)
        self.bn = nn.BatchNorm2d(out_channels)
        self.gelu = nn.GELU()

    def forward(self, x: torch.Tensor) -> torch.Tensor:
        x = self.conv(x)
        x = self.bn(x)
        return self.gelu(x)

class ConvBlock(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, in_channels: int, out_channels: int):
        super().__init__()
        self.block = nn.Sequential(Conv(in_channels, out_channels), Conv(out_channels, out_channels))

    def forward(self, x: torch.Tensor) -> torch.Tensor:
        return self.block(x)

class Flatten(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self):
        super().__init__()
        self.pool = nn.AvgPool2d(kernel_size=7)
        self.gelu = nn.GELU()

    def forward(self, x: torch.Tensor) -> torch.Tensor:
        x = self.pool(x)
        return self.gelu(x)

class Unflatten(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, in_channels: int):
        super().__init__()
        self.conv = nn.ConvTranspose2d(in_channels, in_channels, 7, 7, 0)
        self.bn = nn.BatchNorm2d(in_channels)

1.2.2Out-of-Distribution Testing

The model was trained only at $\sigma=0.5$. I reuse the same ten test digits and one fixed noise tensor while varying the scale, and compare the first- and fifth-epoch checkpoints at each scale.

At low noise levels, the digits remain easy to recover. Around the training scale the denoiser suppresses most background noise while preserving the strokes. Training at one noise level does not teach the network how to adapt explicitly to arbitrary scales.

2.1–2.3Time-Conditioned Flow Matching

Two FCBlocks embed scalar time through Linear → GELU → Linear layers. Their outputs modulate the unflattened bottleneck and first upsampling features. For training, I sample independent noise and a uniform time for each digit, form an interpolated image, and predict the constant path velocity. At each checkpoint, I sample the same 50 seeds (180–229) with 50 Euler updates.

\[x_t=(1-t)x_0+t x_1,\quad t\sim U[0,1],\qquad \mathcal L=\mathbb E\|u_\theta(x_t,t)-(x_1-x_0)\|^2.\]

After one epoch, many samples are fragmented or difficult to read. By epochs 5 and 10, more images form coherent digits, though broken strokes and ambiguous shapes remain. Sampling repeatedly updates the image, allowing the model to follow a time-dependent velocity field instead of predicting the dataset average in one step.

The time-only model estimates a marginal velocity field, $u^*(x_t,t)=\mathbb{E}[x_1-x_0\mid x_t,t]$: a posterior-weighted average of sample-specific conditional velocities. At early, noisy states, several digit identities may be plausible, and their velocity contributions can favor different stroke patterns. Without a class label to narrow these possibilities, accurately learning the direction that will eventually resolve into a clear digit is more demanding. Errors in the learned velocities can accumulate over the 50 Euler updates, leaving mixed or broken strokes. This provides a plausible explanation for some of the ambiguous samples. Accurately learning and integrating the marginal field can still produce sharp digits, while class conditioning helps narrow the possible digit identities.

Reflection

What I Learned

In Part A, I used a pretrained model to implement the basic operations of a flow model. I learned how a predicted velocity determines the next image state, and how repeated Euler updates turn local predictions into a complete sampling process. Comparing Gaussian denoising, one-step estimates, velocity visualizations, and iterative reconstruction helped me connect the equations to visible changes in image structure and detail.

I also gained a clearer understanding of the difference between sample-specific conditional flows and the marginal velocity field learned by the network. This explained why a local prediction need not recover the exact path of a particular source image and why the velocity must be updated during sampling. Image editing showed how starting time and guidance control source preservation and prompt influence, while visual anagrams and hybrid images demonstrated how combining velocity predictions can create different interpretations.

These experiences provided the foundation for Part B, where I learned the concrete structure of a simple flow model by writing my own PyTorch model classes. Building a UNet with skip connections and adding time and class conditioning helped me understand how the architecture supports velocity prediction. I then worked through the full training and sampling pipeline, from generating noisy states and constructing targets to optimization, checkpoints, and Euler sampling. Comparing the pure-noise denoiser, time-only model, class-guided model, and learning-rate experiments helped me connect the training objective and implementation choices to the quality of the final digits.
